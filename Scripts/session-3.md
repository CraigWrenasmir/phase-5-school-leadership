### Slide 2

Let’s begin with communication. Speech is one way of communicating, and we’ll be looking at a much wider picture: how students receive information, how they share what matters to them, and the ways we make both possible.
The amount someone speaks doesn’t tell us how much they understand. A student may need different ways to understand an instruction, answer a question, ask for help, request a pause or say no. All of those messages matter. Communication access belongs in school design and school systems.
We’ll talk about practical options, including augmentative and alternative communication, or A A C. There isn’t one approach that suits every student. Individual assessment and intervention questions belong with the student’s speech pathologist or relevant team; our focus here is what educators can notice, make available and change.

### Slide 3

Let’s come back together. We move between ways of communicating all day, often without noticing. Writing, facial expression, gesture, sign, a communication device and speech are all real communication. One method isn’t more valid because it is spoken.
That gives us something useful to notice at school. When we say, ‘Use your words,’ are we overlooking a message a student has already communicated? A point, a look, or moving closer or further away can be worth attending to. Our job is to listen across the ways that person communicates, while checking what a message means rather than assuming.

### Slide 4

There are two parts to keep distinct here: understanding and expressing. Understanding is information coming in and making sense. That might be a spoken instruction, a written sequence, a demonstration, or a gesture in context. Expressing is a message going out: an idea, a feeling, a question or a choice, through any communication method.
These don’t necessarily look the same for one person. Someone may speak at length and still need support to understand spoken information. Someone else may understand much more than they can readily express. Neither fluent speech nor limited speech gives us the whole picture. Communication can also change with the person, the place, the demands and how someone is feeling.
A A C means augmentative and alternative communication. It can include gesture, signs, a communication board or book, typing, or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech and some instead of speech.
Repeated or scripted words can carry meaning too. Listen for the context and possible purpose before correcting the form. We don’t need to assign a language-processing label before respecting a student’s communication. If we need an individual assessment, we involve the relevant team. For our school practice, the question is: what does this environment help the student understand, and what ways does it give them to respond? Look at routines, documents and expectations too. Where do they assume that speaking is the usual way to participate?

### Slide 5

As we come back, hold onto that specific idea. There is no single Autistic communication profile, so we need to listen to what helps the particular person.
One practical point to carry into school is availability. A student’s communication system needs to be where communication happens: in class, at break, during transitions and when plans change. It cannot be limited to one activity or a therapy session. We support the student’s ways of communicating, working with the student, their family and the relevant team. We aren’t prescribing a system from a workshop example.

As leaders, ask how that access continues across staff, places and routines. Who needs to understand the student’s communication, and how will useful information reach them?

### Slide 8

The first option is clear communication. Use familiar, specific words and give one idea or step at a time, in a form and amount the person can access. That might mean making a direction shorter or making the first step clearer.
The second is processing time. After giving information, pause. A person may be making sense of what you said, organising a response and communicating it. If we fill every pause with a new version of the question, we may add more information before the earlier information has been processed.
The third is visual support: showing information as well as saying it, so there is something to return to.
These are options to match to the person and the moment, rather than a checklist that guarantees success. Keep the student’s A A C available and accept their preferred ways of responding, including refusal. A useful communication support makes messages easier to understand and share. It doesn’t require the student to produce the answer or communication style we were expecting.

When these supports are expected across the school, a student’s access is less dependent on one staff member. Leaders can ask where that consistency already exists and where staff need information or support.

### Slide 9

Visual supports are information you can see and come back to. The three examples here answer different questions. A timer can show how much time is left. A sequence can show what to do next. A daily schedule can show what is happening today. You may already use a calendar, a shopping list or a reminder on your phone in much the same way.
The format needs to make sense to the person. Objects, photographs, written words and symbols can all be options, on paper or on a device. Check that the student understands what the information represents and can reach it when it is needed. A support displayed across the room may exist without being useful at the moment of need.
Think of the visual information already available across your school, including timetables, signage, assemblies and notices home. What question does it answer? Could a student return to it without asking an adult? A visual support offers information; it isn’t a condition the student must satisfy to access something they enjoy.
Visual supports and A A C can overlap, but they aren’t interchangeable. Keep the person’s communication methods available as well. We’ll return to this in Session Four when you choose a design option for a school system.

### Slide 10

Double empathy asks us to look at understanding in both directions. When people have different experiences and ways of communicating, it can be harder for them to understand one another. That doesn’t mean every social misunderstanding sits inside the Autistic student.
Consider a classroom discussion, group task or playground interaction. Whose expectations are shaping what counts as joining in? Who is expected to adjust? What would make this interaction clearer or safer for the people involved?
For example, an adult might expect a quick spoken response as a sign of connection. A student might connect through a shared activity, through detailed talk about an interest, or with more time between turns. Those are possible differences to explore, not conclusions to impose on a particular child.
The question is how each student connects. Connection doesn’t need to look one way. Our expectations and the context shape the interaction too, so we have something useful to examine and change. We can make room for different ways of being together while continuing to listen to what each person actually wants.

Which school practices favour one social style? Think about how groups are formed, what is recognised at assemblies and what counts as participation. These are expectations a leadership team can examine with the people who experience them.

### Slide 12

A student’s social world can include family, school, community and online spaces. Connection might be one to one, playing or working alongside someone, sharing a great deal about an interest, or seeing someone only occasionally. Being together quietly can matter as much as a lively conversation.
Culture also shapes expectations about interaction and relationships. We need to be careful about treating the pattern most familiar to us as the measure of meaningful connection for everyone.
There may be spaces outside home and school where a student feels a particular sense of belonging: a club, a community activity, or another shared-interest space. There may be online connections too. We don’t need to disclose a student’s online activity, identity or community membership to recognise that these possibilities exist.
Keep the picture individual. What do we know about this student’s preferred people, places and ways of joining? What are we assuming? What could we find out respectfully?

### Slide 15

Think of a student’s available energy as a battery. Each day starts with some charge, and it isn’t necessarily the same amount as yesterday. Activities and demands draw on that energy. As the battery runs lower, managing the next demand can become harder.
You may also hear people in the Autistic community talk about spoons. The idea is having a limited supply available: an action uses one spoon or several, and when spoons are low, there is less left for the next thing. These are ways of talking about capacity, rather than a measure we can calculate for someone else.
The other part of the picture is recharging. The right supports can reduce demand and help restore some capacity. A pause, useful information, a preferred way to communicate or a safe connection may make a difference, depending on the person.
So we’ll look for both sides in a school day: what draws on capacity, and what helps to restore it. What helps one student may be different for another.

### Slide 16

Capacity fluctuates. Like all of us, Autistic students can have easier days and harder days. Stress, energy, sensory demands and what else is happening in life can change what is manageable.
That means the same student may do something one day and need more support with it the next. Yesterday’s success doesn’t prove that today’s difficulty is a choice, and finishing a task doesn’t tell us how much effort it required.
Hold that in mind when you look at the students you know. We can ask what is different today, what demands have already accumulated, and what support is available now. We don’t have to know every cause before making information clearer or allowing a useful pause. We do need to keep checking with the student and the people who know them, instead of treating capacity as fixed.

### Slide 21

A student can appear fine without feeling fine. Quietness, compliance or completed work doesn’t give us a full account of what someone is managing. Masking brings that question into our thinking about identity and belonging.
Hold four questions in mind. What might this student be working hard to manage or hide? Where can they be recognised and included? What supports belonging without requiring them to appear less Autistic? And what does the student want others to understand?
We can’t answer those questions simply by watching from the outside. They invite us to listen and to examine the conditions around the student. Include what feels safe and joyful, the connections they value, and places where they can be themselves.
This is a reason to look beyond whether a task was finished or a lesson was quiet. Access matters, and so do the effort involved and the student’s experience of belonging.

Leaders cannot know a student’s experience by watching from an office, but they can shape conditions where different ways of participating are accepted. Ask what the school can do to reduce the need to hide difficulty. If this reflection feels personal, you are welcome to step out and return when you are ready.

### Slide 23

Let’s widen the picture to Autistic staff. The thinking we have used about sensory load, masking, communication and energy also matters in staffrooms and meetings. Your school may include Autistic staff who have disclosed that, and staff who have not. Their access and flourishing matter in their own right.

### Slide 26

Across Sessions Two and Three, you’ve been building a picture of those students. How the world arrives through their senses. How each thinks, focuses and builds understanding. Their strengths and interests. Their internal experience. How they reach towards other people, and what that can cost when the conditions don’t support them.
Managing a school day can involve sensory demands, social interpretation, executive effort and communication across differences. Sometimes it also involves working to appear fine when fine is not what is happening. That effort uses resources.
A child who knows who they are, and feels valued for it, has more of those resources available for learning, connection and self-knowledge. The research discussed in this workshop connects a positive sense of Autistic identity with better mental health and wellbeing. Belonging matters. So does agency: meaningful choice about how to communicate, where to be, and when to step back.
Knowing that other Autistic people share your experience can matter too. There are Autistic adults and communities in which people find belonging and purpose. These students are not alone.
This belongs within our educational work. We can make room for identity, connection and self-understanding in the conditions we create every day. For leaders, belonging, agency and connection belong in the school’s culture and routines, and in the way staff are supported. Take that thought with you into the break.
The deepest thing you can do is support them to know themselves, and to know that who they are is worth knowing.
