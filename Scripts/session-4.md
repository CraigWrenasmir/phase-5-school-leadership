### Slide 3

Our first leadership input is culture. What staff say about students when they are not in the room is influenced by what leaders model and allow to become familiar.
Think about staffroom language. If a student is described as being difficult, a leader might gently ask what the lesson or the day is demanding of them. That is an illustrative response, not a story about a particular school. Repeating that kind of question can make curiosity part of ordinary practice.
What gets celebrated also matters. Naming a specific inclusive practice gives people something they can recognise and learn from. For example, you might acknowledge a clear change-warning routine and the access it supports, instead of offering only general praise.
Now consider a vision staff can say. Privately, write one sentence about inclusion that any staff member could explain. Keep it connected to what students actually experience. What would someone see or hear when that sentence is put into practice?
Remember Autistic staff as well. The culture should make it safer to be oneself, with no expectation to disclose. Keep this reflection about patterns and processes, not individual colleagues. Pause for a moment to write your sentence.

### Slide 4

Our second input is resourcing. Where money and staff time go expresses a school’s priorities. Think privately about the question at the bottom of the slide: who decided the current support staff allocation, and when?
We do not need budget figures or identifiable staffing arrangements here. The useful question is whether an allocation still responds to need, or whether it has continued through habit. Your school-day map and audit can help you ask where demand builds and where support is useful.
Also ask whether access depends on one scarce resource, such as one support staff member, while the wider system remains difficult to use. Warning time, a stable timetable and a useful template can sometimes reduce unnecessary demand with little cost. They still need care, planning and review.
Good records of everyday practice support planning and the school’s N C C D and funding processes. Follow your own governance, staffing and funding arrangements when making formal decisions. For now, connect a resource question to what the map and audit suggest about students’ access.

### Slide 5

### Slide 11

Professional learning and coaching asks what happens after a day like today. Where can staff ask questions, observe practice, try something and reflect on what students experience?
The slide proposes learning that continues through the year, coaching and mentoring in classrooms, time to plan and review together, and a mentor for every early career teacher. The structure will differ across schools. What matters is making a place for the learning to continue in practice.
For a primary example, a teacher might invite a colleague to observe a change-warning routine, then review it together at a team meeting. In a secondary example, a faculty might use part of a meeting for staff to share an adjustment they tried and what they noticed. These are illustrations, not a model everyone must adopt.

### Slide 12

Predictability includes clear information about routine and change. It does not require every day to be identical. Ask whether every student, and every relief teacher, can answer four questions. What is happening? What is changing? What comes next? What can I do if the plan changes?
Consider a primary assembly moved earlier than usual. How will students learn that the plan has changed? Is the change reflected in the information they use, and can they find out what happens next? In a secondary example, a class has both a relief teacher and a room change. Is there a dependable way for students to learn that before they arrive, and does the relief teacher have what they need?
Relief-day plans, warning arrangements and timetable stability involve school decisions. Return to your audit: when something changes, how do students find out? Is the information accessible when it matters, or does a person need to know whom to ask?
Your design could make that route clearer. Test it on an ordinary day and on a day when plans change, using student and staff feedback.

### Slide 13

An inclusion team and a review cycle can make adjustments more dependable. The idea is a small team that plans and reviews on a schedule, so review also happens before something goes wrong.
There are four decisions to make. Who needs to be part of the team? How often will it meet? How will students and families contribute? How will decisions reach every teacher who needs the information, including relief staff?
The membership and frequency will depend on your school and system. Start with the question on the slide: who reviews adjustments now, and how often? What is already clear, and what depends on someone remembering to ask?
Review can confirm that an adjustment remains useful, suggest a change or show that a particular support is no longer needed. Base that judgement on experience, consultation and evidence. A schedule is useful when it creates a real opportunity to hear what is happening and act on it.

### Slide 16

Before you finish the design, let’s apply a Universal Design for Learning lens. Can you make important information clear and available? Can you offer workable ways to participate and respond? Can you support engagement while keeping meaningful challenge in the learning?
Knowledge of individual students and universal design can inform each other. Something you understand about one student’s access may reveal a barrier worth reducing more broadly. A broadly available support may then help several students in different ways.
But the presence of a resource doesn’t guarantee access. Offering another format only helps if students can find it, understand it and use it. We’ll now examine your emerging design with four practical questions, then test it through use and feedback.

At school level, this can shape policies, templates, timetables and shared spaces as well as individual lessons. Keep that wider application in view as you check the design.

### Slide 20

Support is a continuum, rather than one intervention that answers every need. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines, clear expectations, accessible information, and a classroom culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.
Then consider universal supports available to many students. Instructions can be offered in spoken, written and visual forms. Students may have flexible ways to demonstrate learning, with movement breaks or sensory tools available when useful.
Targeted support responds to a more specific need. For example, a small group might benefit from key vocabulary being introduced before a lesson, or from additional organisation support during independent work.
Individual adjustments respond to the particular student’s access and participation needs. Examples might include a quieter workspace for an assessment, additional processing time, a personalised task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning. What is appropriate depends on the student and the context, informed by consultation and evidence.
Look back at the design check. What is available to everyone? What might need to be targeted? What needs individual adjustment? Academic adjustments sit within this wider picture. A diagnosis alone doesn’t tell us which adjustment will help. Connect the response with your observations, the day map, and student and family knowledge, then review it through use and feedback.

For each layer, ask who decides, who delivers and who reviews in your school. A useful continuum also needs clear responsibilities, so the support reaches students and can be adjusted when their needs or circumstances change.

### Slide 21

Reasonable adjustments are changes or supports that help students access and participate in education in a way that works for them. They should be individualised, build on strengths, remove barriers, and be practical and effective. Informed decisions and review help us find out whether they are enabling the student.
The diagram places adjustments within the wider work of environmental design and Universal Design for Learning. It also names the four N C C D levels of adjustment: quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary, substantial and extensive.
Keep two things distinct. Reasonable adjustments are the changes and supports provided. The N C C D is the process for collecting information about adjustments for students with disability. Evidence of what has been provided and monitored matters; a diagnostic label by itself doesn’t choose an adjustment or tell the whole story.
Within curriculum learning, we want support to build capability, confidence, self-advocacy and independence. The question is whether students can access and participate, with support that empowers them. For formal requirements and decisions, use current official guidance and your school or system processes.
